## Expanded Basin Notes

### Hudson Bay Basin (UBI 277) — Detailed Assessment

The Hudson Bay Basin is the largest remaining basin without any CCS activity (1,597,536 km²). Key considerations:

**Petroleum Exploration History:** Approximately 30 exploration wells drilled (1960s–1980s), including the TRI (Test Release Investigation) well program. Sparse 2D seismic coverage. Oil and gas shows encountered in Paleozoic carbonates, but no commercial discoveries.

**Potential Storage Formations:** Ordovician–Devonian carbonate units (Churchill River Group, Shamattawa Formation, Bad Cache Rapids Group) are the primary reservoir targets. Estimated porosity 5–15% in dolomitized intervals. Thick evaporite seal potential (Prairie Evaporite equivalent, Upper Silurian salts).

**Jurisdictional Complexity:** Four provinces/territories (Manitoba, Ontario, Quebec via Nunavik, Nunavut) plus federal (CER) regulatory overlap. No single authority has CCS jurisdiction. Nunavut has no CCS or pore space legislation. Land claims under the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement Act would require Inuit consent.

**Viability Assessment:** Extreme remote logistics (Churchill, MB is the only port), no existing CO₂ pipeline infrastructure, no major stationary emission sources within 500 km except minor mining operations. CCS is not viable under current conditions.

### Michigan Basin (UBI 399) — Detailed Assessment

**Canadian-Side Emitters:** The Sarnia-Lambton industrial complex (Ontario) emits approximately **25 Mt CO₂ per year** from petrochemical refining, chemical manufacturing (NOVA Chemicals, Imperial Oil, Shell, Suncor), and natural gas processing. This represents one of the largest concentrated emission sources in Canada without CCS.

**Ontario Regulatory Readiness:** Ontario has **no CCS Act** and no pore space tenure regime. The province participated in the Ontario Carbon Storage Research Initiative (OCSRI, 2009–2015), which produced geological assessments of southwestern Ontario storage potential but did not advance to regulatory development. Ontario's cap-and-trade program (canceled 2018) briefly included CCS protocols.

**US-Side Activity (Michigan):** Core Energy, LLC operates CO₂-EOR in the Northern Niagaran Pinnacle Reef trend (Silurian reefs), injecting ~1.5 Mt CO₂/year for enhanced oil recovery. The US DOE CarbonSAFE program has funded characterization of Michigan Basin storage (Midwest Regional Carbon Sequestration Partnership, MRCSP). US EPA Class VI well permit applications are pending for Michigan injection sites.

**Geological Favorability:** The Canadian portion of the Michigan Basin contains Silurian pinnacle reef and Devonian carbonate reservoirs analogous to producing US-side CO₂-EOR fields. Estimated storage resource in Ontario saline formations: 20–50 Mt (low confidence, limited characterization). The basin's 337,560 km² area includes both deep saline aquifers and depleted hydrocarbon pools.

**Prognosis:** The Sarnia emitter inventory makes this the strongest candidate among zero-project basins for future CCS, contingent on Ontario enacting CCS legislation.

### Lake Superior Basin (UBI 342) — Expanded Note

The Midcontinent Rift System underlying Lake Superior contains extensive tholeiitic basalt flows (Keweenawan volcanics) up to 20 km thick. These basalts present a **carbon mineralization** opportunity analogous to:

- **CarbFix (Iceland):** In situ basalt carbonation injecting CO₂-charged water into basaltic lava flows at Hellisheidi (~95% mineralization within 2 years).
- **Wallula Basalt Project (Washington State, US):** US DOE-funded pilot injection of ~1,000 tonnes CO₂ into Columbia River Basalts.
- **Basalt CO₂ mineralization research** is active at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL).

No basalt-targeted CCS research, pilot projects, or feasibility studies have been conducted on the Canadian side. Conceptual work (GSC Open File 6582) addresses CO₂ hydrate stability, not mineralization. Canadian-side viability is low due to offshore location (Lake Superior), lack of nearby CO₂ sources, and cross-border complexity with US states (Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan).

### Georgia Basin (UBI 256) — Added in v2.0

The Georgia Basin (Evenick UBI 256; Forearc type, On-Offshore) underlies Vancouver Island, the Georgia Strait, and the Lower Mainland of British Columbia (Metro Vancouver). This basin was not included in the original v1.0 analysis and is added here.

**Emission Context:** Metro Vancouver generates approximately 16 Mt CO₂/year (2023 data) from industrial sources, transportation, and natural gas consumption. Point-source emissions include the Parkland Refinery (Burnaby), Lafarge cement (Richmond/Delta), and several natural gas power plants. The City of Vancouver and BC have carbon neutrality targets but no CCS-specific initiatives in this basin.

**Regulatory Status:** BC has no CCS Act. However, BC's *Carbon Capture, Utilization and Storage Protocol* (2023) provides a regulatory framework for CCS on Crown land, managed through BCER. Pore space ownership is unresolved — BC treats pore space as Crown property but has no tenure issuance mechanism. The *Environmental Assessment Act* applies to major CCS projects.

**No CCS Activity:** Zero Tier 1–2 projects, zero pore space tenure applications, zero IAAC referrals, zero NRCan CCS Fund awards. No known Tier 3 assessments.

## Basin-Type Barrier Breakdown

Barriers differ materially by basin type. The following breakdown contextualizes why CCS remains absent.

### Passive Margin Basins (Labrador, Georges Bank, Banks, East/West Baffin Bay, Davis, North Baffin Bay, Orphan, Scotian, Sydney)

- **Primary barrier:** Deep offshore (>200 m water depth), extreme distance from emission sources, and no existing hydrocarbon production infrastructure for most (except Scotian, Labrador).
- **Secondary barriers:** No regulatory pathway for offshore CCS on Canada's Atlantic and Arctic margins. C-NLOPB jurisdiction limited to NL offshore — no CCS-specific regulations exist.
- **Tertiary barriers:** Marine boundary disputes (Greenland, France/St-Pierre, US), no transboundary CCS agreements.
- **Prognosis:** Arctic passive margins (Baffin, Banks, Lincoln) are decades from CCS viability. Atlantic margins (Labrador, Georges Bank) would require major offshore regulatory reform.

### Intracratonic Basins (Hudson Bay, Moose River, Michigan, Foxe, Prince Regent, McClintock)

- **Primary barrier:** Geographic isolation from emission sources (Hudson Bay, Foxe, Prince Regent) or lack of regulatory framework despite proximal sources (Michigan, Moose River).
- **Secondary barriers:** Sparse geological characterization — very limited drilling for most. Hudson Bay has ~30 wells but zero modern storage assessment.
- **Potential:** Michigan Basin (Sarnia emitters) and Moose River (James Bay hydro industrial emissions) are the most accessible intracratonic basins. Hudson Bay is effectively inaccessible.
- **Prognosis:** Michigan Basin is the only intracratonic basin with realistic CCS potential within 10–15 years, contingent on Ontario legislation.

### Arctic Basins (North Slope, Lincoln, Banks, East/West/North Baffin Bay, Davis, Prince Regent, Foxe, McClintock, Cumberland, Ungava)

- **Primary barrier:** All barriers apply simultaneously — no emitters, no regulation, no infrastructure, extreme climate, no pore space tenure, Indigenous governance requiring land claims consent, no economic case, sparse geology.
- **Special consideration:** The Arctic Offshore is the most challenging CCS environment globally. No offshore CCS project exists in Arctic waters anywhere in the world (as of 2025).
- **Prognosis:** Zero CCS activity expected for the foreseeable future (2040+). Potential long-term indirect relevance: Mackenzie Delta CO₂-CH₄ exchange research (Mallik hydrate program) could inform Arctic geoscience but does not constitute CCS.

### Forearc / Foreland / Onshore Basins (Georgia, Tofino, Queen Charlotte, Bellingham, Bowser, Whitehorse, Anderson-Peel Plain, Bear Lake Plain, Eagle Plain, Mackenzie Plains)

- **Primary barrier:** Small and/or remote basins with limited reservoir potential. Pacific margin basins (Georgia, Tofino, Queen Charlotte) are structurally complex (accretionary wedge setting) with poor reservoir quality.
- **Secondary barriers:** No CCS regulation in BC, Yukon, or NWT. BC has a CCS protocol but no tenure mechanism. Yukon and NWT have zero CCS governance.
- **Prognosis:** Georgia Basin is the most interesting due to Metro Vancouver emissions, but BC regulatory gaps and poor reservoir quality in forearc settings make CCS unlikely without fundamental policy change.

### Rift Basins (Lake Superior, Ungava, Cumberland, Frobisher)

- **Primary barrier:** Lake Superior has basalt carbon mineralization potential but is a shared international water body with no regulatory pathway. Ungava, Cumberland, Frobisher are Arctic basins with all Arctic barriers.
- **Unique opportunity:** Midcontinent Rift basalts (Lake Superior) offer mineralization-based storage, but this requires a dedicated research program similar to CarbFix (Iceland) or Wallula (US). No such program exists on the Canadian side.
- **Prognosis:** Basalt mineralization in Lake Superior is scientifically promising but practically remote due to regulation, cross-border complexity, and lack of near-term emission sources.

The Michigan Basin (Sarnia-Lambton emitter complex) is the strongest candidate among zero-project basins to potentially host future CCS, contingent on Ontario enacting CCS legislation. The Georgia Basin (Metro Vancouver) faces similar regulatory barriers with added geological uncertainty in a forearc setting.
